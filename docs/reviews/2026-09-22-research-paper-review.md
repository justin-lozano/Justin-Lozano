**Yes — the scope works, with one narrowing, and you can develop the analysis.** Do not try to
measure how much of the increase the global oil shock caused. Four pages cannot carry an
attribution that runs from a geopolitical event through world crude to a Hawaii utility's fuel
costs to one meter. What four pages can carry is the facility's *exposure* — how a fuel-price move
reaches this building's bill, how much of it landed between April and July, and what you can do
about it. Same subject, a claim you can defend.

**Your hook is the sentence you may be treating as background.** Usage falling while blended cost
rises from about $0.32 to $0.44 per kWh. A blended rate is the whole bill divided by kWh, so a
shrinking denominator raises it on its own, before any fuel-cost adjustment moves at all. If your
schedule carries a demand charge and a fixed customer charge, both are largely indifferent to kWh,
and falling usage inflates the blended number mechanically. The first question your paper answers
is therefore: how much of that 12-cent move is fuel, and how much is arithmetic?

**The decomposition is the analysis, and nothing precedes it.** You already wrote that the
statements "still need to be verified to separate fuel-related charges from other billing effects."
Build one table, April through July: kWh, then each line item as your statements name it, in
dollars and in cents per kWh. That table is also your Graph/Chart/Diagram — plot the fuel-related
cents per kWh against the non-fuel cents per kWh across the four months and the argument is
visible in one figure.

**Write the falsification condition before you build the table.** Mine would be: the fuel channel
fails if the fuel-related component per kWh is roughly flat across the four months while the
blended rate climbs — then the story is rate structure and load, not oil. A second test is
external: does the facility's fuel component per kWh track HECO's published adjustment for those
months? If it does not, the transmission channel is not what moved the bill.

**Recommendation is 30 percent of the rubric, and it needs a decision-maker and a decision.** Your
question's second half — "what practical response could reduce costs while maintaining required
operations" — is the right half, and that constraint is what makes it hard: a 24/7 clinical
facility cannot shed load, so the levers are the ones that cut peak demand or displace fuel-exposed
kWh, not the ones that cut hours. The decomposition picks the lever. Name who acts, what they
approve, and what it is worth against the same four months of bills. "Verify the billing and
investigate further" is a research agenda; the rubric wants something somebody can sign.

One thing that would help you rather than cost you: the numbers in the paper are stronger when the
table behind them is committed here, the way your Case 1 cell references were. Your prompt log
carries nothing for this paper yet.

**In order — item 1 sets the scope and items 2–5 sit inside it:**

1. Narrow the question from how much the oil shock caused to how the facility is exposed and what
   it can do; revise the brief so the paper's question is the stated question.
2. Decompose the April–July statements into fuel-related and non-fuel cents per kWh.
3. Write the falsification condition, and the external check against HECO's published adjustment,
   before you read the table.
4. Decide who the recommendation is for and what they approve.
5. Make the decomposition your figure.
