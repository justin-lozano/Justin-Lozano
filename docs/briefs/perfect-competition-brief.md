# Perfect Competition — engagement brief

## The problem
I have 64 available beds to divide among three vegetable crops during a 36-week season in a perfectly competitive market. Tomatoes earn $8,800/bed with a maximum of 20 beds, carrots earn $2,094/bed with maximum of 20 beds. Mesclun earns $2,700 per bed with a maximum of 30 beds. This tells me that the combined crop caps total 70, which is six more than the 64 available beds. I must decide how to divide the 64 beds amongst each vegetable crop as I cannot max each of them out. If revenue was the only consideration, tomatoes would be ideal as they would earn the most per bed. However, based on the compounding labor for tomatoes, the labor is raised by 10% per additional tomato bed, compared to the other vegetables with a rate of 2.5% for carrots and 1.25% for mesclun. Given this information, I am not trying to figure out which crop would earn the most, rather, how many beds should be planted for each respective crop before any extra revenue concentrated in a crop would no longer be worth it. If a poor decision is made, there would be excessive labor costs or lost revenue for the season. This decision needs to be made before any planting begins with little to no opportunity to correct a bad allocation during the season.

## What I am assuming
I am assuming the farm will use all 64 beds and cannot go over any of the limits. Since we are taking the market price, I will assume that the revenue from each bed stays the same. Adding one bed makes all the beds of that crop more labor intensive. I am taking the provided labor estimates of 2.5hrs/bed/week for tomatoes, 0.833hrs/bed/week for carrots, and 1.25hrs/bed/week for mesclun and assuming they accurate. I am also assuming the farmer and temporary worker costs given in the case reflect the actual cost of labor. If I had more time, I would test whether temporary workers will be available and whether yields and revenues remain stable.

## Hypothesis
I expect 18 tomato beds, 16 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds because mesclun earns more than carrots and has the lowest labor-compounding rate. With tomatoes earning the most per bed, I expect the revenue to justify the 18 beds but not the full 20 due to the compounding labor rate of 10%. My estimate of 18 beds reflects my expectation that the rapidly increasing labor requirements will make the final tomato beds less worthwhile. I selected 16 carrot beds because after allocating 18 beds to tomatoes and 30 beds to mesclun, only 16 available beds remain. I expect the higher revenue from tomatoes and mesclun to justify prioritizing those crops over the final four carrot beds.

## How I would know I was wrong
My reasoning would be slightly off if the model recommends 16 or 17 tomato beds, meaning that I have underestimated the effect of its labor compounding rate. If it recommends 15 or fewer tomato beds, I would consider my reasoning materially wrong because i significantly underestimated the compounding effect. If the model recommends 19 tomato beds, I would have slightly overestimated the labor-compounding effect while 20 beds would provide stronger evidence that I overestimated its effect. If the model ends up recommending 28 or 29 mesclun beds, my prediction would be close but would still overestimate the value of maxing out mesclun. If it recommends 27 or fewer beds, I would be wrong in expecting its higher revenue and lower compounding rate to justify maxing it out. If the model recommends more than 16 carrot beds, i have underestimated the value of their lower labor requirements and compounding rate compared with the higher revenue crops.
