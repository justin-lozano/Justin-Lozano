## Reflection
My Stage 1 hypothesis predicted 18 tomato beds, 16 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds. The model instead settled on 10, 20, and 30 where I was correct only for mesclun.

For tomatoes, I overestimated by 8 beds because I reasoned from expected revenue rather than tracing the actual marginal cost curve. My analysis shows marginal cost climbing steadily until the 11th bed, where it jumps to $9,391 against the price of $8,800, which resulted in a loss on that bed. I did not account for how quickly diminishing returns would drive hours/bed back up even after the switch to the cheaper temporary labor rate. Enough additional hours were required by the 11th bed that marginal cost climbed past price again despite the lower wage. I assumed a smoother cost curve than the model actually produced.

For carrots, I underestimated by 4 beds because I applied the same MC-equals-price logic that stopped tomatoes, assuming carrots would hit a similar cost ceiling. Instead, the MC stayed well below price ($1,689 vs $2,094) even at the 20-bed cap. This is the constraint that stopped further planting and not rising cost. This distinguishes a resource-bound crop from a cost-bound crop.

Mesclun landed correctly at 30 beds.  However, I didn't reason through at the time that, like the carrots, mesclun's marginal cost ($2,420) sat below its price ($2,700) at the cap. This tells me I reached the correct number without originally distinguishing a capacity constraint from a cost-based stopping point.

The takeaway from my decision memo is that capacity, not cost, served as the binding factor for two of three crops. My memo's $352/bed carrot expansion value matters because that constraint is what actually limited profits, not marginal cost.
